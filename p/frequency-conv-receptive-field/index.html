Networks That Convolve FFT Coefficients, and Their Equivalence to Hadamard Products in the Original Domain

2026-11 32 minutes read

AI statement: I wrote this article together with AI. The theorems were derived by AI, and the experiment code was written by AI.

1. Research question

Between 2021 and 2026 a number of models first apply an FFT to the input and then convolve the FFT coefficients along the frequency axis, in EEG, speech enhancement, MRI k-space, time series and other areas. In images the representative work is FFC (Chi et al., 2020) and its successors LaMa (Suvorov et al., 2022) and FasterFC (Liu et al., 2023), which take "any operation in the spectral domain has a global receptive field" as a design principle. By the convolution theorem, a circular convolution along the frequency axis is exactly a pointwise multiplication by a window in the original domain (a Hadamard product).

The question studied here is therefore the equivalence between these networks and networks built from Hadamard products in the original (time) domain.

The study follows three directions:

  1. Sigmoid-type activations: the whole network is equivalent to its original-domain form.
  2. ReLU, softmax: the equivalence holds only approximately; we study whether the error can be determined in advance and how it propagates layer by layer. We first expected the error to be determinable in advance for all of these activations, and found that ReLU is unstable.
  3. Practice: we test the equivalence of what the two parameterizations learn when trained from scratch.

The proof proceeds along the line two-layer MLP (different activations) → residual stream + attention → real models.

Let the frame length be \(N\), the number of channels \(C\) and the kernel length \(K\). An ordinary short convolution and the local circular convolution used in neural networks both cost \(O(K C^2 N)\) (a global circular convolution computed directly costs \(O(C^2 N^2)\)). An FFT costs \(O(C N \log N)\), and a Hadamard product costs \(O(C^2 N)\) (\(O(CN)\) without channel mixing). The main cost comes from the \(N^2\) complexity of global circular convolution; the FFT is the cheap step.

2. Direction 1: operator equivalence, two-domain structure and computational complexity

The linear part of a frequency-domain convolutional network is exactly equivalent to a Hadamard product in the original domain; the nonlinear part corresponds, in the original domain, to repeated circular convolutions of the signal with itself, which is a nonlocal operation. The whole network is therefore equivalent, in the original domain, to "an alternating composition of block-diagonal windowing (mixing only channels) and global self-convolution polynomials (mixing only time)".

2.1 Notation and setup

Let \(x \in \mathbb{C}^N\) be a signal in the original domain (indices taken modulo \(N\)). Its discrete Fourier transform (DFT) and inverse are

\[X[k] = (\mathcal{F}x)[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] e^{-2\pi i k n / N}, \qquad x[n] = (\mathcal{F}^{-1}X)[n] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} X[k] e^{2\pi i k n / N}\]

We define three basic operators in the original domain:

  • Hadamard product (windowing operator): \((M_w u)[n] = (w \odot u)[n] = w[n] u[n]\).
  • Circular convolution: \((u \circledast v)[n] = \sum_{m=0}^{N-1} u[m] v[(n-m) \bmod N]\).
  • Conjugate flip: \((R u)[n] = \bar{u}[-n \bmod N]\).

With these conventions the convolution theorem gives a duality in both directions: \(\mathcal{F}(u \circledast v) = U \odot V\), and \(\mathcal{F}^{-1}(K \circledast X) = N (\mathcal{F}^{-1}K) \odot x\).

2.2 Commutative diagrams and the natural isomorphism

With these conventions the convolution theorem is equivalent to the following commutative diagram, where \(w = N\mathcal F^{-1}K\):

\[\begin{CD} \mathbb{C}^N @>{\mathcal F}>> \mathbb{C}^N \\ @V{M_w}VV @VV{K\circledast\,\cdot}V \\ \mathbb{C}^N @>>{\mathcal F}> \mathbb{C}^N \end{CD}\]

This diagram states \(\mathcal F\circ M_w = (K\circledast\,\cdot)\circ\mathcal F\). The left column is the original domain and the right column the frequency domain; carried across horizontally into the original domain, the linear part of a frequency-domain convolution layer is windowing.

More generally, let \(G\) be a finite abelian group and \(\hat G\) its character group (\(\hat G\cong\mathbb{Z}/N\) for \(G=\mathbb{Z}/N\), with \(\chi_k(n)=e^{2\pi i kn/N}\)). We define two covariant functors from the category of finite abelian groups to the category of commutative \(\mathbb{C}\)-algebras:

  • \(A(G) = (\mathbb{C}^G, \circledast)\) is the group algebra. A group homomorphism \(\varphi: G\to H\) acts by pushforward, \((\varphi_* u)[h] = \sum_{\varphi(g)=h} u[g]\).
  • \(B(G) = (\mathbb{C}^{\hat G}, \odot)\) is the algebra of functions on the dual group. \(\varphi\) acts by pullback \(\hat\varphi^* U = U\circ\hat\varphi\) along the dual map \(\hat\varphi:\hat H\to\hat G\), \(\psi\mapsto\psi\circ\varphi\).

The DFT \((\mathcal F_G u)(\chi) = \sum_{g\in G} u[g]\,\overline{\chi(g)}\) is an algebra isomorphism \(A(G)\to B(G)\) (the convolution theorem), and for every group homomorphism \(\varphi\) the following diagram commutes:

\[\begin{CD} A(G) @>{\mathcal F_G}>> B(G) \\ @V{\varphi_*}VV @VV{\hat\varphi^*}V \\ A(H) @>>{\mathcal F_H}> B(H) \end{CD}\]

Since \((\mathcal F_H\varphi_*u)(\psi)=\sum_{g} u[g]\,\overline{\psi(\varphi(g))}=(\mathcal F_G u)(\psi\circ\varphi)\), \(\mathcal F: A\Rightarrow B\) is a natural isomorphism.

When \(\varphi\) is the quotient map \(\pi:\mathbb{Z}/N\to\mathbb{Z}/(N/s)\), \(\pi_*\) is folding (aliasing sum) in the original domain and \(\hat\pi^*\) is sampling with step \(s\) in the frequency domain, \((\hat\pi^*X)[k']=X[sk']\). This is exactly the correspondence "stride ↔︎ folding" in the table of Section 2.3.

A pointwise activation \(\sigma\) in the frequency domain commutes with every pullback: \(\sigma\circ(U\circ\hat\varphi) = (\sigma\circ U)\circ\hat\varphi\). Hence \(\sigma\) is a natural transformation from the underlying-set functor of \(B\) to itself; conjugated by \(\mathcal F\), the original-domain self-convolution operator \(\mathcal P=\mathcal F^{-1}\sigma\mathcal F\) commutes with every pushforward, and in particular with folding:

\[\begin{CD} \mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{Z}/N} @>{\mathcal P}>> \mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{Z}/N} \\ @V{\pi_*}VV @VV{\pi_*}V \\ \mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{Z}/(N/s)} @>>{\mathcal P}> \mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{Z}/(N/s)} \end{CD}\]

Windowing \(M_w\) depends on the particular window and does not have this naturality for a general \(w\).

2.3 Equivalences

Let layer \(l\) of a frequency-domain network be \(Z_l = K_l \circledast H_{l-1}\), \(H_l = \sigma(Z_l)\).

Proposition 2.1 (No-go theorem 1: there is no substitute that avoids the FFT)

If a continuous map is pointwise in both the original domain and the frequency domain, that is \(\mathcal{F}(f(x)) = g(\mathcal{F}x)\), then \(f\) is a scalar multiplication \(f(x) = cx\). Hence any nontrivial pointwise activation \(\sigma(Z)\) in the frequency domain is necessarily nonlocal in the original domain and cannot be reduced to an original-domain Hadamard product.

Proposition 2.2 (self-convolution form of a nonlinear layer)

  • Polynomial activations: if \(\sigma(t) = \sum_{j=0}^d a_j t^j\), write \(u^{\circledast j}\) for the \(j\)-fold self-convolution (\(u^{\circledast 0} = \delta\), the unit impulse at the origin). Then

\[\mathcal{F}^{-1}\sigma(\mathcal{F}u) = \sum_{j=0}^d a_j u^{\circledast j}\]

  • Analytic activations (sigmoid / tanh): when \(\sigma\) is approximated on a bounded interval \([-B, B]\) by a degree-\(d\) Chebyshev polynomial, the error decreases geometrically in \(d\), and this gives an equivalent self-convolution polynomial to any precision.
  • Split real/imaginary activations (e.g. DCCRN): if the activation is \(\sigma(\mathrm{Re}\,Z) + i\,\sigma(\mathrm{Im}\,Z)\), then since \(\mathrm{Re}\,Z = (Z + \bar{Z})/2\) and \(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\bar{Z} = Rz\), the original-domain form generalizes to a bivariate convolution polynomial in \(u\) and \(Ru\) (containing both self-convolutions \(u \circledast u\) and autocorrelations \(u \circledast Ru\)).

2.4 Operator analysis

An \(L\)-layer frequency-domain convolutional network is equivalent, in the original domain, to an alternating composition of two kinds of operators:

\[h_l = \mathcal{P}_l \big( M_{w_l} h_{l-1} \big)\]

where \(M_{w_l}\) is a linear operator that is block-diagonal along the time axis (it mixes channels and does not mix time), and \(\mathcal{P}_l\) is a nonlinear self-convolution operator (it mixes time and does not mix channels).

With frame length \(N\), \(C\) channels, frequency-domain kernel length \(K\) and polynomial degree \(d\), the per-layer costs compare as follows:

ImplementationPer-layer costProperties
Frequency-domain convolutional network (\(K\)-tap convolution + frequency-domain \(\sigma\))\(O(K C^2 N)\)Baseline; the convolution cost grows linearly with the kernel length \(K\).
Original-domain linear layer (Hadamard windowing \(M_w\))\(O(C^2 N)\)Saves a factor of \(K\) in multiplications over the frequency-domain convolution, and the equivalent kernel length is not limited by \(K\).
Original-domain self-convolution polynomial (expanding \(\mathcal{P}_l\))\(O(d C N^2)\) directly\(O(d C N \log N)\) with FFTs, more expensive than computing in the frequency domain directly; used for exact analysis.
Hybrid implementation (original-domain windowing + FFT)\(O(C^2 N + C N \log N)\)The cheapest exact equivalent in practice: it keeps the factor-\(K\) saving of the linear layer and avoids both the polynomial approximation error and the cost of self-convolution.

2.5 Numerical verification

  • Synthetic data: for every linear equivalence, polynomial self-convolution, boundary term, conjugate flip and the attention identity, the relative error between the two sides is of order \(10^{-14}\).
  • Real model DCCRN (public pretrained weights, speech enhancement): all 6 complex convolution layers are exactly equal to original-domain windowing plus correction terms, with maximum relative difference \(3 \times 10^{-15}\). When layer 1 together with its BatchNorm and a polynomial activation is computed entirely in the original domain, the result has a relative difference of \(1.9 \times 10^{-15}\) from the frequency-domain forward pass.

3. Direction 2: error propagation, explosion mechanisms and approximation limits for non-analytic activations

Layer-wise error bounds can be derived in advance and hold rigorously, but on real models the bound is too loose and the actual approximation error is too large, so replacing ReLU-type activations by polynomials is unusable in practice.

3.1 An exact error identity and a classification of explosions

For any input and weights, the output error between the substitute network and the original network at layer \(D\) satisfies the exact identity

\[E_D = \sum_{l=1}^{D} G_l \, r_l\]

where \(r_l\) is the difference between the polynomial and the activation at layer \(l\), and \(G_l\) is the propagation operator formed by the product of "average slope \(\times\) weight" over the subsequent layers.

Proposition 3.1 (single-layer approximation and layer-wise recursion)

  • Single-layer error: for ReLU the error is concentrated in a band of width \(B/d\) around the kink (a neighborhood of 0), and the relative error is controlled by the peak-to-RMS ratio of the pre-activation (bounded by \(0.42 B / (\mathrm{RMS} \cdot d)\), decreasing as \(d^{-3/2}\) in the asymptotic regime). For sigmoid/tanh the error decreases geometrically in \(d\).
  • Layer-wise recursion: \(e_l \le \kappa \delta_l + L \lVert W_l\rVert_2 e_{l-1}\), where \(\lVert W_l\rVert_2\) is exactly the maximum modulus of the original-domain window. When the contraction condition \(c = L\lVert W\rVert_2 < 1\) holds, the error bound converges to a fixed point and does not grow with depth.

Proposition 3.2 (complete classification of errors)

The output error arises through exactly three routes:

  1. Out of range: a pre-activation of the true network leaves the calibration interval \([-B_l, B_l]\) at some layer.
  2. Insufficient margin: let \(M_l = \lVert z_l\rVert_\infty\) be the maximum magnitude of the true network's pre-activation at layer \(l\), so the calibration interval leaves a safety margin \(B_l - M_l\). When the shift \(\lVert \tilde{z}_l - z_l\rVert_\infty\) caused by upstream error (bounded in the worst case by \(\lVert W_l\rVert_2 e_{l-1}\)) exceeds this margin, the substitute network's pre-activation is pushed out of \([-B_l, B_l]\) into the region where the polynomial diverges.
  3. Positive Lyapunov exponent: the Lyapunov exponent of the product of Jacobians of the true network is \(\lambda > 0\), so the propagation operator \(G_l\) amplifies exponentially.

Explosion cases constructed according to this classification were all confirmed numerically. For example, an ordinary sigmoid network does not amplify error because it carries a bias term proportional to the window at the origin, which pushes the activations into saturation. After we set the sum of the kernel coefficients to zero to remove this bias, \(\lambda\) turns from negative to positive and the sigmoid network also shows error explosion.

Polynomial substitution is therefore limited to analytic activations such as sigmoid, tanh and Swish. For ReLU-type models this article keeps only the structural conclusion: the linear part is exactly equivalent, the nonlinear part is a nonlocal operation in the original domain, and there is no practical input-independent polynomial form. What is usable for ReLU-type models is the exact substitution of Section 5 (Proposition 5.0): keep ReLU in the frequency domain and replace only the frequency-domain convolution by original-domain windowing, with zero error.

3.3 Complexity lower bounds for approximation forms (No-go theorem 2)

Any approximation of the frequency-domain ReLU in the original domain by better function forms runs into explicit complexity limits:

  • Multiplication only (composed polynomials): repeatedly composing the cubic \(s \leftarrow s(3-s^2)/2\) (the Newton–Schulz iteration for the matrix sign function) makes the error decrease exponentially in the total number of multiplications (reaching 1% error takes 18 composition steps, against degree 4096 for a single polynomial), and the composed polynomial does not explode within \(\sqrt{3}B\). By the Bernstein lower bound, however, \(b\) bits of precision require a multiplicative depth of at least \(b-3\), so this form cannot be independent of depth.
  • Division allowed (rational functions): an integral representation together with Zolotarev rational approximation brings the depth down to the constant 3, and the rational function never diverges. Division corresponds to deconvolution in the original domain, and on ordinary hardware this is still slower than FFT → ReLU → IFFT.

3.4 Residual stream and attention

  • Residual stream (derivation not yet independently checked): the identity branch changes the coefficient of the absolute error bound to \(1 + L\lVert W\rVert_2 \ge 1\), so in the worst case the error accumulates linearly with depth, while the identity branch also dilutes the amplification of relative error. Pre-norm LayerNorm keeps the error of each row bounded, growing at most linearly.
  • Attention: softmax attention over frequency tokens has an exact original-domain form (convolution exponential + deconvolution normalization), but upstream error passing through attention has only a local Lipschitz constant, which grows quadratically with the feature magnitude; there is no global constant.

4. Direction 3: equivalence under training from scratch, and the domain of the nonlinearity

On two real tasks (ReLU activation), a frequency-domain convolutional network trained from scratch and its original-domain equivalent reach the same task metrics. Removing the frequency-domain nonlinearity, which reduces the model to a pure original-domain Hadamard network, removes its ability to mix samples, and performance collapses.

4.2 Analysis

All three networks have 4 layers, 16 channels, frame length \(N=512\) and hop 256, and are trained from scratch with Adam (learning rate \(10^{-3}\), cosine annealing), 3000 steps, batch 256, 3 random seeds.

  • Denoising: the data are LibriSpeech speech mixed with DEMAND noise, with input SNR 5.04 dB; the test uses 8 unseen speakers and held-out noise segments. The network outputs a residual estimate of the form \(X + f(X)\).
  • Forecasting: the dataset is ETTh1, split 12/4/4 months into train, validation and test; the data are standardized with training-set statistics, and the model is channel-independent. A 512-point frame consists of 416 hours of history and 96 hours of future; the future part of the input is set to zero, instance normalization uses the mean and standard deviation of the history, and loss and metrics are computed only on the 96 future points.

Multiplying the original-domain signal by this window and then taking the FFT differs from a direct 5-tap circular convolution in the frequency domain by a relative difference of about \(5\times10^{-16}\).
  • The two networks have the same parameters, so this comparison tests equivalence of implementation: both learn the same 5 taps and differ only in the path of the forward computation, so results under the same seed are nearly identical (9.94 vs. 9.94, 10.47 vs. 10.46). The remaining differences come from the different order of floating-point operations and accumulate over 3000 training steps. The network with a different parameterization is the "freely learned window per point" of Section 5.2. Its function class is larger (equivalent to a frequency-domain kernel of arbitrary length), and its results differ (denoising 11.65 dB, forecasting 0.481).   • Derivation of optimization invariance: write \(w=AK\), where column \(j\) of \(A\) is \(e^{-2\pi i(j-2)n/N}\), so that \(A^{H}A=NI\). Gradient descent on \(K\) gives the update \(\Delta K=-\eta A^{H}\nabla_w\mathcal L\), hence \(\Delta w=A\Delta K=-\eta AA^{H}\nabla_w\mathcal L=-N\eta\,P\nabla_w\mathcal L\), where \(P=AA^{H}/N\) is the orthogonal projection onto this \(K\)-dimensional subspace. Under SGD the kernel parameterization is therefore the same as the window parameterization restricted to the subspace with learning rate \(N\eta\), and the two trajectories coincide. Adam normalizes each coordinate separately, and the normalization differs between the \(K\) coordinates and the \(w\) coordinates, so this invariance does not hold for Adam.
  • The reason the control gives exactly 6.22 dB: it consists of "pointwise windowing + original-domain pointwise ReLU", so output \(n\) depends only on input sample \(n\), with no mixing between samples. Such a network can at best multiply each sample by an optimal gain. With clean-to-noise power ratio \(\mathrm{SNR}\), the optimal scalar gain \(g=\mathrm{SNR}/(1+\mathrm{SNR})\) gives an output SNR of \(1+\mathrm{SNR}\), which for a 5 dB input is \(10\log_{10}(1+10^{0.5})=6.19\) dB, matching the measured 6.22 dB.
  • The three seeds are identical and more parameters do not help: the optimal per-sample gain has a closed form, and every initialization converges to it. With the parameters increased from 8,000 to 819,200 (a freely learned window per point), the network still reaches 6.22 dB. What this network lacks is mixing between samples, which parameter count cannot supply.
  • The reason the forecasting result equals the window mean: the input at the 96 future positions is 0, and the control's output there depends only on this 0, so it is a constant. After undoing instance normalization this constant is the mean of the history. The measured 0.7077 is identical to the "output the window mean" reference line.
  • The mixing comes entirely from the nonlinearity in the frequency domain: the ReLU of the frequency-domain convolutional network acts on spectral coefficients, and by Proposition 2.2 it is a self-convolution in the original domain, so every output sample depends on every input sample. What does the work in "FFT + convolution" is therefore the nonlinearity applied in the frequency domain; the convolution itself amounts to multiplying by a window. As a mirror-image control, a time-domain convolutional network (5-tap convolution along time + original-domain ReLU) is pointwise windowing in the frequency domain, and its mixing comes only from local convolution along time. This network has 4 layers plus an output layer, 5 convolutions in total, so its receptive field is only 21 samples; it scores 7.80 dB on denoising and 0.685 on forecasting, between the other two.

5. Substitute implementations: the gain of the frequency-domain nonlinearity at lower cost

By Direction 1, what does the work in a frequency-domain convolutional network is "a nonlinearity applied in some basis", and the convolution itself is only windowing. Accordingly, this section keeps original-domain windowing, replaces or simplifies the transform wrapped around the activation, and compares the cost and task performance of one exact substitution and several approximate ones.

5.1 Exact substitution: original-domain windowing + a pair of FFTs around the ReLU

Proposition 5.0 (exact for any pointwise activation in the frequency domain, including ReLU)

Let layer \(l\) of the frequency-domain convolutional network be \(H_l=\sigma(K_l\circledast H_{l-1})\), where \(\sigma\) is any pointwise activation (ReLU, PReLU, ReLU applied separately to real and imaginary parts, sigmoid, etc.), not required to be analytic or polynomial. Write \(h_{l-1}=\mathcal F^{-1}H_{l-1}\) and \(w_l=N\mathcal F^{-1}K_l\). By the convolution theorem \(K_l\circledast H_{l-1}=\mathcal F(w_l\odot h_{l-1})\), so

\[H_l=\sigma\big(\mathcal F(w_l\odot\mathcal F^{-1}H_{l-1})\big)\]

This identity holds pointwise for every input and every weight. The commutative diagram of this layer is:

\[\begin{CD} H_{l-1} @>{K_l\circledast\,\cdot}>> Z_l @>{\sigma}>> H_l \\ @V{\mathcal F^{-1}}VV @AA{\mathcal F}A @. \\ h_{l-1} @>>{M_{w_l}}> w_l\odot h_{l-1} @. \end{CD}\]

  • Only the convolution is replaced; the ReLU stays as it is. The activation acts unchanged on the frequency-domain coefficients and is not approximated by a polynomial. None of the difficulties of ReLU in Direction 2 (approximation error decreasing only as \(B/d\), out-of-range overflow, resting defects, layer-wise amplification) appear here: the error is zero, independent of the calibration data, the input range and the depth.
  • The cost is one extra pair of FFTs per layer. By Proposition 2.1 a nontrivial pointwise activation in the frequency domain is necessarily nonlocal in the original domain, so this pair of transforms cannot be removed; the FFT costs only \(O(CN\log N)\), cheaper than the \(K\)-tap convolution it replaces.
  • Division of labor with Direction 2. Direction 2 examines writing the whole network entirely in the original domain, and for ReLU the answer is negative (polynomial substitution is unusable). Proposition 5.0 examines replacing the frequency-domain convolution by an original-domain Hadamard product without changing the function, and for ReLU the answer is positive, provided the activation is still applied in the frequency domain.
  • Numerical check: the two forms of the linear part differ by a relative difference of about \(5\times10^{-16}\). Trained from scratch with ReLU, the two implementations reach nearly identical task metrics under the same seed (denoising 9.94 vs. 9.94 dB, forecasting MSE 0.4322 vs. 0.4359), with the differences coming from the order of floating-point operations.
  • Form on real models: models such as DCCRN use zero padding, stride, and a separate BatchNorm for the real and imaginary parts. Proposition 5.0 still holds, with \(w_l\odot\) replaced by the original-domain operator with correction terms from Direction 1 (windowing plus boundary terms, folding, conjugate flip), and the cost has to be recomputed. The linear part of this form has been verified on DCCRN to \(3\times10^{-15}\); this article has not yet compared task metrics after replacing the whole network.

Proposition 5.1 (per-layer cost and the condition for being cheaper)

Assume that in both implementations the features are already in the frequency domain when entering layer \(l\) (the previous layer's activation was applied in the frequency domain), with \(C_{in}\) input channels and \(C_{out}\) output channels. The unit of counting is a complex multiplication; an FFT of length \(N\) is counted as \(N\log_2 N\) (radix-2 actually takes about \(\tfrac12 N\log_2N\), so this count is conservative).

The multiplications of the two implementations are counted step by step:

  • Frequency-domain convolutional network: each frequency bin \(k\) of each output channel is \(\sum_{c'}\sum_{j}K_{cc'}[j]\,H_{c'}[k-j]\), which takes \(K\,C_{in}\) multiplications; with \(N\,C_{out}\) output values the total is \(K N C_{in} C_{out}\).
  • Exact substitution, in three steps:
    1. one IFFT for each of the \(C_{in}\) input channels: \(C_{in}N\log_2N\);
    2. original-domain windowing with channel mixing, a \(C_{out}\times C_{in}\) matrix-vector product at each time point: \(N C_{in}C_{out}\);
    3. one FFT for each of the \(C_{out}\) output channels: \(C_{out}N\log_2N\).
ImplementationMultiplications per layer
Frequency-domain convolutional network: frequency-domain \(K\)-tap convolution → frequency-domain \(\sigma\)\(K N C_{in} C_{out}\)
Exact substitution: IFFT → original-domain windowing → FFT → frequency-domain \(\sigma\)\(N C_{in} C_{out} + (C_{in}+C_{out}) N \log_2 N\)

The two compute the same function. The exact substitution saves \((K-1)NC_{in}C_{out}\) and pays an extra \((C_{in}+C_{out})N\log_2N\), so it is cheaper if and only if

\[(K-1)\,\frac{C_{in}C_{out}}{C_{in}+C_{out}} > \log_2 N\]

With equal input and output channels (\(C_{in}=C_{out}=C\)) the condition becomes \((K-1)\,C > 2\log_2 N\), and the speedup is

\[\frac{K N C^2}{N C^2 + 2CN\log_2 N}=\frac{K\,C}{C+2\log_2N}\]

The more channels, the closer the speedup is to \(K\): windowing itself takes \(K\) times fewer multiplications than a \(K\)-tap convolution, and the FFT cost is only proportional to \(C\), so it is amortized by the \(C^2\) channel mixing.

  • The cost of the exact substitution is independent of the kernel length \(K\): windowing always costs \(NC_{in}C_{out}\). The longer the kernel, the more the exact substitution saves; a freely learned window per point is equivalent to a frequency-domain kernel of length \(N\) at the same cost.
  • Layers with few channels and short kernels do not pay off (for example \(C=4\), \(K=5\) and \(C=8\), \(K=3\)); such layers should keep the frequency-domain convolution.
  • Depthwise convolution (each channel convolved only with itself, no channel mixing, as in Conformer): the frequency-domain convolution costs \(KNC\) and the exact substitution \(NC + 2CN\log_2N\), a speedup of \(K/(1+2\log_2N)\) independent of the number of channels. At \(N=512\), \(K=31\) the speedup is only 1.63.
  • All of these counts are multiplication counts. Short convolutions have highly optimized kernels on GPUs, while the FFT has fixed and memory-access overheads, so the ratio of actual running times has to be measured.
  • By Propositions 2.1 and 3.3 the pair of transforms around the activation cannot be removed, and direct computation with original-domain polynomials or rational functions costs more. Under the constraint "the function is unchanged", the exact substitution is the cheapest implementation of its kind.

Cases where the exact substitution is slower. The exact substitution trades the convolution cost \((K-1)NC_{in}C_{out}\) for the cost of a pair of transforms \((C_{in}+C_{out})N\log_2N\). When the convolution is already cheap, the trade does not pay off:

  • Very short kernels: with \(K=1\) (a \(1\times1\) convolution that only mixes channels) the convolution itself is \(NC_{in}C_{out}\), so the exact substitution only adds cost and is always slower. At \(N=512\) with equal input and output channels, the minimum number of channels for each kernel length is:

    Kernel length \(K\)23571131

  • Few channels: for layers with a single channel on one side (the first layer, the output layer) the condition becomes \((K-1)\,C/(1+C)>\log_2N\), so at 16 channels the exact substitution needs \(K\ge11\) to be cheaper. With the usual 3–5 taps such layers should keep the convolution.

  • Depthwise convolution: depthwise convolution has no channel mixing, so the speedup is \(K/(1+2\log_2N)\), and at \(N=512\) the exact substitution needs \(K>19\) to be cheaper.

  • Longer frames raise the threshold: the threshold grows with \(\log_2N\). From \(N=512\) to \(N=4096\), the channels needed for 2 taps rise from \(C>18\) to \(C>24\), and for 3 taps from \(C>9\) to \(C>12\).

  • Two-dimensional kernels: DCCRN's kernel is 5 along frequency × 2 along time. Windowing replaces only the frequency direction; the 2 taps along time remain a convolution that both implementations pay for, so the speedup is computed with \(K=5\) along frequency, with both sides multiplied by the factor 2 along time.

  • Zero padding and stride: here the original-domain operator is windowing plus boundary terms and folding (Direction 1), and the extra correction terms have to be counted separately; this article has not recomputed this cost.

  • Actual running time: all of the above are multiplication counts. Short 3–5-tap convolutions have highly optimized kernels on GPUs; the FFT has a fixed overhead and more memory traffic, more noticeably at small batch sizes. Layers that only just pay off by multiplication count may therefore run slower in practice.

In practice a designer should therefore choose layer by layer: switch to the exact substitution only on layers where both the kernel length and the number of channels are large enough, and keep the frequency-domain convolution elsewhere. The two forms compute the same function, so mixing them does not change the network.

5.3 Analysis

  • The exact substitution matches the frequency-domain convolutional network on both tasks with 43% of its multiplications. It is the only substitution that holds up on both tasks.
  • The effect of the Haar transform and of the free window depends on the task: on denoising the two are 1.0 dB and 1.45 dB higher, and on forecasting they are 0.31 and 0.05 worse. One explanation, which has not been derived, is this: denoising needs a basis in which speech is sparse so that coefficients can be kept or discarded pointwise, and a local Haar basis is enough; forecasting needs to move information from 416 known points to 96 future positions, which only a global transform such as the FFT can do. The free window overfits on forecasting.
  • The time-domain convolutional network has a receptive field of only 5 samples per layer; it barely reaches the future interval in forecasting, and it is 2.4 dB below the frequency-domain network on denoising.
  • The failure of the windows modulated by global inner products on forecasting is caused by the experimental design: the 96 future positions of the input are set to zero, so a network that only multiplies outputs zero there, and it did not get a fair test.

6. Further results

6.1 Polynomial closure and expressive power

  • Frequency-domain convolution and original-domain Hadamard products are dual to each other and exactly closed under linear operations. Polynomial activations are also closed between the two domains: a degree-\(d\) pointwise polynomial in the frequency domain corresponds to a \(d\)-fold self-convolution in the original domain, with finitely many terms.
  • ReLU and sigmoid are not closed; their original-domain forms need infinitely many terms. This is directly tied to expressive power: a non-polynomial activation is necessary and sufficient for universal approximation by feedforward networks (Leshno, Lin, Pinkus, Schocken, 1993), and the expressive power of polynomial networks can be described by the dimension of algebraic varieties (Kileel, Trager, Bruna, 2019).
  • In homomorphic encryption there is work replacing ReLU by composite minimax polynomials (arXiv:2105.10879, ResNet-152 accuracy 77.52% vs. 78.31%), and work allocating polynomial degree according to each layer's input distribution (arXiv:2310.10349). The latter is close to this article's approach of distribution-weighted fitting with per-layer degree allocation.

6.2 Three kinds of error

  • Approximation error: this error is caused by replacing the activation with a polynomial, and it is what Direction 2 studies.
  • Optimization error: this error is caused by training not converging to the optimum, and it is unrelated to the equivalences here.
  • Rounding error: this error is caused by floating-point arithmetic. A high-degree polynomial grows as \(T_d(|t|/B)\) outside the interval; in double precision at \(d=128\) the rounding part is more than ten orders of magnitude larger than in exact arithmetic, so the degree should be no higher than the target precision requires.

The degree to which forward propagation amplifies error is the conditioning (Jacobian gain ÷ signal gain). Backpropagation uses the transpose of the same Jacobian, so a network that amplifies error in the forward pass also amplifies gradient error in the backward pass.

7. Open problem: the placement of the local operator relative to the global operator

One open problem is kept here: using this architecture to explain where the local operator is placed relative to the global operator. The current experimental papers are:

  • Conformer (Gulati et al., 2020): the paper runs ablations only on LibriSpeech. Placing the convolution after attention works best, but only 0.1 WER better than placing it before, with no variance reported, so the difference may be random fluctuation.
  • Squeezeformer (Kim et al., 2022): the paper argues that a large-kernel convolution directly after attention is redundant, but does not compare the two orders.
  • Branchformer (Peng et al., 2022): the paper runs the two in parallel and learns the merge weights. It observes that in shallow layers the two branches take turns and in deep layers attention dominates, but this is only an empirical observation with no explanation.

Conformer is exactly the reverse case: it convolves along time, which is equivalent to taking an FFT along the time axis and then multiplying pointwise by a window in that frequency domain.

Seen in that frequency domain, Conformer's convolution only multiplies each frequency component by a coefficient, acting as a filter that does not mix different frequency components. The mixing comes from what follows it, namely the Swish activation and attention.

Our experimental conclusion says the same: in a frequency-domain convolutional network the convolution itself is only windowing, and what makes every output depend on every input is "the nonlinearity applied in the other domain". So discussing "convolution before or after" is in essence discussing the order of windowing (filtering) and the step that brings mixing (the nonlinearity, attention). This gives the empirical question an angle from which it can be derived.

This is therefore an open problem.

Another open problem is whether some simple compositions can make a model learn what a convolution expresses without performing the convolution.

8. Limitations

  • All experimental networks are small (4 layers, 16 channels) and all fall short of the least-squares linear model on forecasting; the ranking shows only the difference between mixing schemes and says nothing about which network suits forecasting.
  • Each task has a single dataset; training is fixed at 3000 steps with no early stopping.
  • Direction 2 was measured on only two ReLU-type networks.
  • All costs are derived multiplication counts; running times were not measured.
