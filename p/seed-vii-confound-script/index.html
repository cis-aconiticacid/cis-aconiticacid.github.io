Emotion EEG Datasets Teach Models to Recognize Videos

2026-10 13 minutes read

SEED-VII is a widely used emotion EEG dataset. Twenty participants watched the same 80 video clips over 4 sessions of 20 clips each, and the experimenters recorded EEG while they watched. Before the recordings, the dataset authors assigned each clip to one of 7 classes according to the emotion it was designed to elicit: 12 clips each for happiness, surprise, sadness, disgust, fear and anger, and 8 neutral clips. The dataset gives all 20 participants the same label for the same clip, and that label records which emotion the clip was designed to elicit. Researchers use these data to train models that recognize emotion from EEG, and they value cross-subject evaluation most: researchers train a model on 19 participants, test it on the remaining one, and rotate through all 20. This split is called leave-one-subject-out. Every clip the test participant watched was also watched by the other 19 participants in the training set, under the same label. A model that can tell from the EEG which clip is playing, and then retrieve that clip's label from the training set, answers correctly without using emotion at any step.

I tested 10 published models on SEED-VII and measured, model by model, how much of each cross-subject score comes from recognizing the clip. Each model with a high reported score has its own route to the label that bypasses emotion: some recognize the clip from recording length, one recognizes it from a single time window of EEG, and one writes the label itself into its input features. Models without such a route stay between 20% and 30% accuracy, where chance for 7 classes is 14.3%. In the literature, most papers that report cross-subject results on these datasets use splits that allow this kind of scoring.

Models recognized the video, not the emotion

The 80 clips in SEED-VII vary widely in length. When computing the official features, the dataset authors cut each recording into 4-second time windows, so one clip spans 13 to 87 windows, from 52 seconds to about 6 minutes. The 80 clips take only 44 distinct lengths, and the same clip has almost the same number of windows across participants: 19 participants match exactly, and the remaining one differs by at most 2 windows. Recording length therefore works like a fingerprint. A model that knows how long a recording is knows roughly which clip it is. I ran a lookup experiment that ignores EEG entirely and uses only length: under the leave-one-subject-out split, I predicted for each length the most common emotion among training clips of that length. This reached 67.5% accuracy on 7 classes, while always guessing the most common class reaches 15%. I then switched the test to clips absent from training and guessed from the known clip with the nearest length, and accuracy fell to 25%. The 67.5% therefore comes almost entirely from length identifying the clip.

The official features carry a second cue. To compute the de_LDS features, the dataset authors first computed differential entropy for every time window, channel and frequency band, then smoothed it with a linear dynamical system. The smoothing spans the whole recording, so every window's values mix in information from earlier and later in the same recording, and the windows of one clip resemble each other.

The same structure runs through the whole SEED family and FACED. SEED has 15 participants and 15 film clips of about 4 minutes; the experimenters showed the same 15 clips in all 3 sessions, in the same order. FACED has 123 participants, who all watched the same 28 clips. The SEED-VII authors also reused SEED-family clips for 5 of its classes: happiness, sadness, neutral, disgust and fear. In these datasets the label is a property of the clip. It states which emotion the clip was designed to elicit and is the same for everyone who watched it. A model that recognizes the clip has the label.

Separating clip recognition from emotion recognition

I used three tests to separate the two.

The first test uses fake labels. I randomly reassigned the official labels of the 80 clips among the clips, gave each clip the same fake label for all 20 participants, and kept the number of clips per class unchanged. I made three such reassignments. Fake labels have no correspondence with emotion, so a model can learn them only by remembering which fake label belongs to which clip. After a reassignment, one fake class may by chance hold more clips of a particular true emotion, so a model that knows only emotion can still score above chance on fake labels. To remove this part, I computed for each model an emotion-explained score: I took the predictions of the same model trained on true labels, mapped each true emotion to the fake class containing the most clips of that emotion, and compared the result with the fake labels. This score is what the model's own emotion recognition earns when carried over to the fake task. Any part of the model's actual fake-label score above it can come only from recognizing clips. I compared the two scores pair by pair on the same test participants and the same samples. For a model whose per-sample predictions were not saved, I used the emotion ceiling as the reference instead: the highest fake-label score an ideal model could reach if it knew every sample's true emotion but could not recognize clips.

The second test removes length. I cut every recording to the same length, or switched off the model component that carries length, and checked whether the score dropped.

The third test holds out clips. I removed a whole group of clips from training and tested only on those clips the model had never seen. I also ran a control: the control removed a different group of clips with exactly the same emotion composition, so the amount of training data was the same while the test clips were ones the model had seen. The difference between the two is the score that comes from having trained on the test clips.

I did not use the test set to choose training epochs; I read out each model at the end of training. FAT is the one exception: its authors' code rolls the model back to the best test accuracy inside the training loop. I kept that behavior, and true and fake labels went through the same pipeline. Accuracy means 7-class classification accuracy throughout.

How the four high-scoring models score

PR-DA (IEEE BIBM 2025) reports 50.43%. Its released code covers only SEED, and I built the same pipeline on SEED-VII from that code: the code treats each recording as one sample and pads the sequence of 1-second differential entropy frames with zeros at the end, up to 265 frames. The number of zero frames the code adds tells the model how long the recording is. This pipeline reached 95.3% at full length and 92% to 94% on the three sets of fake labels, while the emotion-explained score is only 27% to 29%. When I cut every recording to its first 50 seconds, which flattens the length information, accuracy fell to 40.2%. The paper's 50.43% cannot come from this code, because the code fails on recordings longer than 265 seconds; SEED-VII has 8 such clips, and I added the truncation. These experiments show that the data handling in the authors' released code hands the recording length directly to the model.

EmoVAE (CogSci 2026) also takes whole recordings as input and, as the paper describes, pads the sequence of 1-second differential entropy frames with zeros to 265 frames. Following the paper's protocol, I obtained 34.8%. On fake labels it exceeds its emotion-explained score by 10 points on average, and 53 of 57 pairs come out higher, so it recognizes clips. When I cut recordings to their first 50 seconds, accuracy fell to 21.9%, consistent with length being one of its cues for recognizing clips.

FAT (MICCAI 2025) reports 51.12%. It takes as input a single de_LDS vector from one 4-second window, 62 channels by 5 frequency bands, so the model cannot see recording length. The authors' code runs leave-one-subject-out separately inside each of the 4 sessions, and each session has only 20 clips. I obtained 48% to 56% on true labels. On fake labels it reached 40% to 52%, on average 24 points above its emotion-explained score, and 237 of 240 pairs came out higher. FAT shows that recognizing clips does not need length: the EEG in a single 4-second window is enough to tell which of a session's 20 clips is playing.

The authors' code for HEART (IEEE TAFFC 2026) outputs 89.7%. HEART cuts each recording at a set of segment boundaries, extracts features such as covariance, distances between segments and speed of change from the segments, and passes them to a random forest. It chooses the segment boundaries according to the recording's true emotion label, for test recordings as well, so the label is written directly into the features. I reproduced 90.0% with the authors' code and their participant split, and 92.1% with 5-fold cross-validation over participants. When I chose the boundaries according to fake labels instead, the model learned the fake labels at 84% to 92% accuracy. When I switched to fixed boundaries unrelated to any label, true-label accuracy fell to 21.6% under 5-fold cross-validation over participants, and the fake-label score was on average only 2.7 points above the emotion-explained score. HEART's high score comes from the label carried in its features.

Models that do not recognize clips stay at 20% to 30%

EEG-FM-Bench (ICML 2026) is a benchmark for pretrained EEG models. On its SEED-VII task I ran four pretrained models, CBraMod, LaBraM, BIOT and REVE, with the benchmark's own configurations and the official weights, using 15-second raw EEG windows as input. My CBraMod result matches the one the benchmark paper reports. The four models reach 20% to 27% accuracy, and their fake-label scores are essentially equal to their emotion-explained scores, with every three-set average difference within 1.5 points. When I held out clips, BIOT lost 2.8 points because it had not seen the test clips, in the same direction in all 5 folds, and on unseen clips it still scored about 5 points above chance. The differences for the other three models were not statistically significant.

LGF (EMBC 2026) reports 40.1%, and I obtained 29.1% at the end of training. Its fake-label score differs from its emotion-explained score by less than 1 point, and when I cut every recording to its first 52 seconds, neither the true-label nor the fake-label score changed. MAT (TAFFC 2026) reports 34.49%, and I obtained 21.5% at the end of training; on fake labels it exceeds its emotion-explained score by only 0.9 points on average across the three sets.

The LGF and MAT papers report the best epoch on the test set. Under that practice, both models also score 6 to 8 points higher than at the end of training on fake labels that carry no emotional information; choosing epochs on the test set picks these points out of training noise.

Every model with a reported score above 50% has a route that bypasses emotion. After I removed that route, the models' cross-subject accuracy on SEED-VII fell to between 20% and 30%, the same range where the models without such a route sit. This is the amount of emotion information that the models I tested can read from EEG and carry over to new participants.

The same splits run through the literature

I checked 152 papers from 2018 to 2026 that recognize emotion on SEED, SEED-IV, SEED-V, SEED-VII and FACED, and recorded the evaluation split of each one. Of these, 101 report at least one result in which the test clips appeared in training. After I set aside the 25 papers whose splits are unclear, the proportion is 80%. Among the 36 papers at ICLR, NeurIPS and ICML, 25 fall into this group. Most of these results come from cross-subject splits. Across all 152 papers, an evaluation that is both cross-subject and clip-disjoint appears only 3 times. On SEED-VII and FACED, no cross-subject result is clip-disjoint. The most common participant split on FACED comes from CBraMod (ICLR 2025), and more than ten later papers adopted it.

A split that allows clip recognition leaves open whether a model uses it, and researchers have to test each model; on SEED-VII I saw both cases. Several further signs point the same way. Among the models with the highest reported cross-subject scores on SEED-VII, EmoVAE and MMSTG-Net (EEG plus eye movements) both take whole recordings as input, where recording length can enter the model. On FACED, INCEPT classifies whole 30-second recordings and reports 84.75% class-averaged accuracy on 9 classes, while pretrained models that classify time windows under the same split reach 55% to 58%. No one has tested this gap yet. Some methods also use the shared stimuli directly as a training signal: they treat EEG from different participants at the same moment of the same clip as positive pairs for contrastive learning, then run cross-subject evaluation on the same clips. mdJPT (NeurIPS 2025) and CLISA are examples.

On this basis I have a reasonable suspicion that a whole group of papers reporting cross-subject results on these datasets carry some clip-recognition component in their scores, to varying degrees. To settle how much each one carries, researchers need to run fake-label and held-out-clip tests on the corresponding model. The SEED-VII results show that this component ranges from close to zero to almost the entire score.

The label belongs to the clip

In emotion EEG datasets, labels come from the designed category of the stimulus, and all participants share the same stimuli, so recognizing the stimulus and recognizing the emotion are indistinguishable at the level of the label. Researchers treat the cross-subject split as the harder setting and the one closer to real use; on these datasets, that split puts every test clip into the training set. Recording length, features smoothed over the whole recording, and choosing epochs on the test set each give models a further route around emotion. In this structure, a cross-subject accuracy mixes emotion recognition and stimulus recognition, and that number alone cannot separate them.

The means to separate them are already available. Researchers can evaluate with test clips that never appear in training, for any participant. The official SEED-VII 4-fold split holds out 5 clips per session in every fold and meets this condition, and researchers can also add held-out clips on top of a cross-subject evaluation. When researchers report results, they can also report scores on clip-level fake labels and compare them pair by pair with the emotion-explained score. Researchers can make all samples the same length, or remove length-related quantities from the input, and compare scores before and after cropping. When researchers choose training epochs or roll back a model, they can rely only on a validation set drawn from the training participants. Dataset maintainers can publish an official clip-disjoint split, release features that are not smoothed across time windows, and state plainly that each label is the clip's designed category.

On SEED-VII, cross-subject emotion recognition evaluated this way currently reaches 20% to 30%. When researchers compare new methods starting from this level, they compare how well a model reads emotion from EEG.
